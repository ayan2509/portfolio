Context

By 2020, 622 million Indians were online, nearly half of them in rural India, and every one of them on a mobile phone.

Who's coming online? Nine in ten new users prefer to read and write in their own language. For most of them, a low-cost smartphone is their first way onto the internet: to learn, to stream, to pay and to reach public services.

Stuck on QWERTY. Almost everything we do on a phone involves typing: a comment, a post, a search, a form. But the keyboard defaults to English, and English isn't everyone's first language. For first-time users from rural areas, that alone can shut them out of much of what the phone offers. Transliteration lets you type your own language in English letters, but only if you already know those letters well enough to spell it with them.

Sources: IAMAI & Kantar, ICUBE 2020 ↗ · KPMG & Google, 2017 ↗ · Google, Gboard internationalization, 2019 ↗

This project looks at how Indic scripts were written, mechanised and adapted to computers, reviews how people type in them today, and proposes an interface for text entry in Indic languages, suited to modern smartphones.

The Script

Designing a keyboard starts with understanding how the scripts work. Their letters look different, but most share the same underlying logic.

Characteristics of indic scripts

क · ক

Abugida system

Every consonant comes with an inherent vowel, and which vowel depends on the language. क reads "ka" in Hindi, while ক reads "kô" in Bengali.

कि · की · कु · के

Vowel signs

To change the vowel, you add a mark called a matra, not a new letter. It can sit before, after, above or below the consonant.

क्ष · ক্ষ

Conjuncts

Consonants can fuse into a single new shape, in Hindi, Bengali, Telugu and most other Indian languages. Some barely resemble their parts.

क ख ग घ ङ

Phonetic nature

You write what you say. Consonants are grouped by where in the mouth the sound is made, and children learn them in that order: the varnamala.

That logic is elegant on paper, but on a keyboard built around the English alphabet it shows up as four recurring problems.

Design challenges when building a keyboard layout
47 Indic
26 English

Large character sets

Over 47 base characters won't fit on one small screen, so keyboards hide them behind shift screens and long presses.

कConsonant + ⇧Shift + ◌ीMatra = की

Multistep input

A single syllable can take three taps: the consonant, a shift key to find the matra (vowel), then the matra itself.

कConsonant + ◌्Virama + षConsonant = क्ष

Conjuncts

Most keyboards build conjuncts with a silencing mark (virama), so you have to know how a shape is made before you can type it.

बहगदजड परकतचट मनवलसय

Unfamiliar layouts

Every keyboard arranges the letters its own way, and few follow the varnamala people already know.

None of these problems is new. Every generation of Indic keyboard, from the typewriter to the smartphone, has had to find its own way around them.

Evolution of Indic Keyboards

Every Indic keyboard has had to decide how to arrange its keys: by the phonetic nature of the script, or by how often each letter is used.

Frequency

Frequency-based layout

The most-used characters go on the easiest keys to reach. Other characters are typed using the "Shift" key. Optimized for the QWERTY layout.

Phonetic

Phonetic layout

Keys follow the script's own order, the varnamala, based on the phonetic structure of the language. Vowels and consonants are grouped separately.

Godrej Typewriter

  • Letters placed by how frequently they're used
  • Over 50 characters squeezed onto keys designed for 26
  • Two characters per key; matras used a dead key (one that types without moving forward)

InScript Keyboard

  • Key positions set by how frequently each letter is used
  • Two characters per key, to fit the QWERTY layout

Every keyboard layout was a product of its time. For smartphones, the first step was to test the status quo: Gboard and SwiftKey.

The Status Quo

Most people type on Gboard (Google) or SwiftKey (Microsoft), while Swarachakra, from IIT Bombay, takes a phonetic approach.

  1. 01

    Shift screens

    Hindi spreads its letters across four screens, Gujarati two and Bengali none, though each has 34–39 consonants. For multilingual users, every switch means learning a new layout.

    • Gboard
  2. 02

    Dynamic vowels

    Tap a consonant and the vowel keys update with each matra attached, enhancing typing efficiency.

    • Gboard
    • SwiftKey
  3. 03

    Typing mechanisms

    Gboard adds handwriting, and both offer swipe typing. Nice to have, but neither makes typing noticeably faster.

    • Gboard
    • SwiftKey
  4. 04

    Phonetic grid

    Developed by the Industrial Design Centre, IIT Bombay, this keyboard uses a phonetic layout for easy alphabet discovery, but at the cost of screen space.

    • Swarachakra
  5. 05

    The chakra

    Tapping a consonant opens a wheel of its vowel combinations, often covering the keys around it. To keep the wheel on screen, no consonants sit at the edges.

    • Swarachakra

Each keyboard solved part of the problem. The next step was to combine what worked in a new layout.

Layout Explorations

Each exploration looked for an intuitive way to arrange the keys, one that stays consistent across languages so the learning curve isn't too steep.

The radial wheel ran into the chakra's problem: keys near the edge pushed it off screen, and working around that made the keyboard taller.

The Final Layout

The final layout was shaped by four principles, balancing the natural order of the script with the frequency of each character.

The design principles
  1. 01

    Logical structuring

    Keys follow the alphabetical order of Indic scripts, the order people learn in school, so letters are easy to find.

  2. 02

    Frequency of use

    Frequently used characters sit on the first screen. Less common ones wait on alternate screens or in long-press popups.

  3. 03

    Categorical integrity

    Vowels, consonants, symbols and numbers each keep their own group, which cuts down on hunting.

  4. 04

    Aesthetics and symmetry

    Consistent spacing makes the layout feel familiar. Colour-coded vowels and a separator between vowels and consonants keep it balanced and easy to scan.

The Keyboard in Use

Built as a working Android app, the keyboard puts the principles into practice in everyday typing, with one layout logic across languages.

  • Vowels, consonants and numbers up front. Rarer characters are just a tap or a long press away.

  • Pick a consonant and the vowels rearrange around it, putting every syllable one tap away for faster typing.

  • One keyboard for every language you write in, with the same logic in each.

  • Bring your conversations to life with a full range of emoji.

Impact & Outcomes
In people's hands

People found the keyboard easier to navigate and faster to type on than keyboards like Gboard and SwiftKey.

92% found the layout intuitive

Colour-coded vowels and the alphabetical grouping let people spot letters at a glance, without hunting across screens.

30% felt faster to type on

Self-reported after three days of everyday use, compared with how fast they typed on Gboard and SwiftKey.

78% praised the language switch

Mostly from people fluent in several languages, for whom moving between Hindi, Bengali and English is part of everyday typing.
